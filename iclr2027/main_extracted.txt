ABSTRACT
Post-training quantization can preserve answer accuracy while changing the com-
putation that selects between retrieved context and parametric memory. We test
this possibility with JuICE, a dual-run attention-head intervention, on Llama-
3.1-8B and Gemma-2-9B base models under GPTQ INT4. We identify source-
arbitration heads on FP16, lock them, and test forward transfer, random-head con-
trols, and in-precision application on 100 held-out conflict items for Llama and
the original 50-item set for Gemma. On Llama, an FP16 context-head set trans-
fers head-specifically to INT4 (+15percentage points (pp), 95% CI[+8,+23]),
and native INT4 K10 reproduces the effect (+16pp[+8,+24]). The locked FP16
parametric pair is detected at INT4 at a narrow operating point (−4pp[−8,−1]
atα=1), against four controls; higher gain produces scorer collapse rather than
stronger mechanistic evidence. On Gemma, the directional ranking misses the
raw positive parametric peak: steering that single head transfers head-specifically
from−12pp[−22,−4]on FP16 to−8pp[−16,−2]on INT4, whereas a random-
head control is exactly zero. The same ranking blind spot appears in both fami-
lies. A Gemma context-suppression effect is detected at both precisions but is not
distinguishable from its locked random-Kcontrol, illustrating why intervention
efficacy alone is insufficient evidence of circuit transfer. Thus, quantization trans-
fer is neither uniformly preserved nor determined by source category; circuit-level
claims require direct controls of intervention specificity.
1 INTRODUCTION
When retrieved context contradicts a model’s parametric memory, a small set of attention heads
can be used, at test time, to steer whose word the model trusts (Li et al., 2025). This paper asks
whether that residual-stream geometry is an FP16 object or a property of the model that survives
compression: if JuICE is computed on FP16, does it still steer after GPTQ INT4, even on items
whose facts survive quantization? Accuracy can fall from forgetting, terse decoding, or a change in
source arbitration; a transferred intervention distinguishes those cases. Figure 1 is the test: identify
causal heads on FP16, verify them by dual-run steering, then apply the locked heads to GPTQ
INT4, on Llama-3.1-8B and Gemma-2-9B base (Grattafiori et al., 2024; Rivi`ere et al., 2024), after a
behavioral check on Llama Instruct.
The contribution is a causal, head-level transfer test under GPTQ INT4 (Frantar et al., 2023), not a
new accuracy table. Llama context K10 transfers head-specifically atn=100, and its native INT4
counterpart reproduces the effect; the locked Llama parametric pair is detected only at the narrow
α=1operating point and fails four independent controls at that gain. Gemma’s raw parametric peak
also transfers, while its context-suppression effect is not distinguishable from a locked random-
head control. Behavioral efficacy is not, by itself, evidence that the same causal object survived
compression.

2 RELATEDWORK
When retrieved context contradicts parametric memory, a language model must decide whose word
to trust (Xie et al., 2024). JuICE (Li et al., 2025) is a test-time, training-free dual-run intervention
on a small set of attention heads. The original JuICE models are base checkpoints, not Instruct,
a distinction developed in Methods (§3.2). We do not revive a memory-head versus context-head
dichotomy (Jin et al., 2024); the transfer test is whether a locked intervention remains the same
causal object after GPTQ. Prior compression work measures recall without a conflicting passage
(Wang et al., 2026) or RAG accuracy without constructed conflicts (Yazan et al., 2024). Neither
tests whether an FP16-identified arbitration intervention still acts on the same object after INT4.
A drop in closed-book recall, or a stable RAG score, can occur without that circuit surviving. We
double-filter on facts that remain answerable at both precisions and then transfer locked JuICE.

5 DISCUSSION
The two families reject a category-level account of transfer (Figure 5). Llama now shows two
narrow, specificity-confirmed INT4 effects atn=100: context K10 forward/native transfer and the
locked parametric pair atα=1. The latter disappears into scorer collapse at higher gain. Gemma’s
transferable object remains the raw parametric peak L27H6 on the original 50-item set, while direc-
tional context K10 enlarge is a FP16 ceiling null. The evidence supports neither “context survives
compression” nor “parametric memory breaks.” Rather, whether a particular causal object transfers
is model- and direction-dependent under this GPTQ recipe.
The cross-family regularity is methodological. In both families, the directional scorepar pos−
ctx poselevates context suppressors above the strongest positive parametric signal. On Llama,
checking the raw peak did not recover a useful INT4 parametric steer; on Gemma, checking it
discovered the only detected FP16-to-INT4 parametric transfer. Directional top-Kshould therefore
not be treated as interchangeable with a source-specific circuit without inspecting the underlying
positive score and applying an intervention.

6 CONCLUSION
If a source-arbitration intervention is computed in FP16 residual-stream geometry, GPTQ INT4 does
not apply a uniform tax to that object. On Llama-3.1-8B base, context K10 and the locked parametric
pair both show narrow, head-specific transfer atn=100, but at different gains and with different
control profiles. On Gemma-2-9B base, the raw parametric peak transfers on the original 50-item
set, while context enlarge is a ceiling null. The surviving object is therefore model-, direction-, and
operating-point-specific, not determined by source category. In both families, directional ranking
promotes context suppressors over the strongest positive parametric signal, and a detected output
effect is not the same as a detected identified-versus-random contrast. Circuit-level claims about
quantization therefore require the identify×apply design, a random-head control, and a look at the
completions—not a source label and a same-sign∆.
